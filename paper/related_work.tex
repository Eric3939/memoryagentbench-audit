\paragraph{Agent-memory benchmarks.}
LoCoMo \citep{maharana2024locomo} and LongMemEval \citep{wu2025longmemeval} test recall over long multi-session conversations; LongMemEval does so with 500 questions spanning five abilities. MemoryAgentBench (MAB) \citep{hu2026memoryagentbench} turns long-context datasets into incremental multi-turn streams. It scores four competencies: accurate retrieval, test-time learning, long-range understanding, and conflict resolution (originally called selective forgetting). Later benchmarks extend the setting. MemoryArena \citep{he2026memoryarena} couples memory to interdependent agentic tasks and finds that agents near saturation on LoCoMo do poorly there. BEAM \citep{tavakoli2026beam} scales conversations to 10M tokens. MEME \citep{jung2026meme} adds multi-entity and dependency tasks, on which all evaluated systems collapse. Most of these papers, MAB included, report a single run per system without repeats or intervals. MEME is the exception, with a repeated-run appendix discussed below. We ask which comparisons MAB scores can actually resolve.

\paragraph{Critiques and audits of memory evaluation.}
Audits so far target labels, judges, scoring rules, and confounds, mostly on LoCoMo and LongMemEval. An audit of LoCoMo found 6.4\% of the answer key wrong and a judge that accepts most deliberately wrong answers \citep{penfield2026locomoaudit}. The Mem0/Zep dispute \citep{chhikara2025mem0,rasmussen2025zep,mem0zep2025issue,zep2025liesblog} shows one system reported on LoCoMo at 58.44\%, 65.99\%, 75.14\%, and 84\%, depending on who ran it and how Category-5 questions were counted. ConvoMem \citep{pakhomov2025convomem} argues that LongMemEval's smallest categories are too small for significant conclusions. Anatomy of Agentic Memory \citep{jiang2026anatomy} shows that F1-based and judge-based rankings disagree on LoCoMo and that judge scores shift with the judge prompt. In MemDelta's one-factor ablations on LongMemEval-S \citep{wang2026memdelta}, swapping only the embedding model reverses the Mem0-versus-RAG verdict. Chanhnourack \citep{chanhnourack2026auditing} re-judges answers, swaps readers, and repeats passes on LongMemEval-S, and concludes that one-answer margins between top systems are smaller than judge disagreement. MemStrata \citep{yadav2026memstrata} reports that grading protocol alone moves LoCoMo accuracy on identical answers from 78.25\% to 90.91\%. Panthi and Abdelfattah \citep{panthi2026sameranking} show that changing the credited scoring target alters retrieval scores on 83--94\% of queries and flips system orderings. Sheverev et al. \citep{sheverev2026beyond} show that a leader's advantage disappears under retrieval-budget matching and that top systems form statistical ties. The only reanalysis of MAB itself, by Pakhomov and Nijkamp \citep{pakhomov2026bookkeeping}, covers one split, conflict resolution. A frozen last-write rule answers 80.25\% of its questions, and 67 items have gold answers the rule cannot reach (a third of the multi-hop questions at 262K). We use their reachability split as a covariate. We differ by auditing published MAB claims across competencies and by measuring stochastic variance rather than label or definitional validity.

\paragraph{Variance and reliability of agent and LLM evaluation.}
Miller \citep{miller2024errorbars} treats evaluation questions as draws from a super-population and recommends clustered and paired standard errors. For agents, Bjarnason et al. \citep{bjarnason2026randomness} find that single-run SWE-bench pass@1 varies by 2.2--6.0 points depending on which run is picked, and that temperature 0 does not make runs deterministic. Mustahsan et al. \citep{mustahsan2025stochasticity} separate between-query and within-query variance with the intraclass correlation. Rabanser et al. \citep{rabanser2026reliability} propose reliability metrics including run consistency and prompt robustness. On BFCL, Chen et al. \citep{chen2026noisefloor} find temperature-0 reruns nearly deterministic, while semantics-preserving prompt perturbations produce much larger paired spread. AlHamidi et al. \citep{alhamidi2026videoqa} rerun one paired agentic comparison and see its sign reverse. Generalizability theory (G-theory) decomposes these sources. Messing \citep{messing2026hidden} decomposes LLM pipeline error over items, prompts, judges, and replicates. Hardy et al. \citep{hardy2026agentreliability} apply Bayesian G-theory to agent leaderboards and note that execution noise cannot be separated without repeated runs. Anand \citep{anand2026askwhich} combines G-theory with an audit of published claims on LLM-judged benchmarks and finds all 17 recovered MT-Bench improvements below that benchmark's measured floor. It is the closest precedent for our claims audit. Within memory, MERIT \citep{mishra2026merit} reports seed gaps of up to 0.45 for embedding retrieval. Ground Truth First \citep{spencer2026groundtruth} uses three replicates and finds that rankings invert as history grows. MEME \citep{jung2026meme} reruns ingestion and answering five times for four LLM-based systems and finds overall standard deviations of at most 0.02, with rankings preserved. As MEME's systems are far apart, we frame our measurements as calibrating the noise floor for close comparisons, not as overturning rankings. None of these works separates rebuild variance from read-time variance, and none treats ingestion order as a facet.

\paragraph{Order and construction effects in memory.}
Long-context models use information unevenly by position \citep{liu2024lost}, so order can matter even without explicit memory. RoutePrism \citep{xu2026routeprism} builds memory in forward and reverse order from the same records and traces which evidence survives. Restoring a single displaced record recovers over 60 points of lost accuracy. Its policies are largely deterministic and construction is not repeated under a fixed order. Self-improving memory agents depend strongly on task order, with default orderings acting as a hidden curriculum \citep{ye2026fragility}. Zhang et al. \citep{zhang2026usefulmemories} find that the same trajectories yield qualitatively different consolidated memories under different update schedules. Deng and Sinchaisri \citep{deng2026stale} find that out-of-order arrival lowers retrieval quality in a keyed personal-fact store and that ordering by stated time restores it. Hu and Ramachandran \citep{hu2026trustgap} vary stated recency and position orthogonally and find that the position effect changes sign with model scale. For rebuilds, the closest evidence is an anecdote in a systems paper \citep{rajah2026postgraphrag}: identical code scored 75\%, 70\%, and 60\% on 20 LongMemEval items purely from re-indexing. These studies are diagnostic, single-system, or outside MAB. Our experiment makes order and same-order rebuild crossed variance components of MAB scores and rankings, across long-context, BM25, embedding-RAG, and Mem0 agents. It runs on FactConsolidation, where recency is stated by serial number, so permuting the ingestion order leaves the gold labels valid. We then use the measured floor to re-read the 147 published MAB improvements in our audit.

\paragraph{Benchmark integrity.}
Benchmark scores can be invalidated by the evaluation code itself. Checklists and audits of agentic benchmarks
\citep{zhu2025abc,tu2026benchguard,gao2026supportscores} and construct-validity reviews \citep{bean2025construct}
document flawed tasks, graders and harnesses. For memory benchmarks specifically, a scoring defect in LoCoMo's
reference code leaves a share of questions unscorable \citep{tanguturi2026atant}, and the Mem0/Zep dispute turned
on how one question category was counted \citep{mem0zep2025issue}. Our harness finding is of this kind, but it
affects the \emph{input} side: Mem0 is queried under a different key from the one its memories were stored under,
and Cognee's store mixes contexts.
